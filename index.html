The theory in 30 seconds

Sharpe's Capital Asset Pricing Model (1964)

In 1964, William Sharpe (Nobel Prize 1990) asked how risky assets should be priced. His idea: investors can remove the risk of a single company by holding many stocks, so only the risk you cannot diversify away, market risk, should be rewarded. Market risk is measured by beta: when the market moves by 1%, a stock with a beta of 1.5 moves by 1.5% on average.

The CAPM in one line

Extra return of a stock = beta × market premium (7.2% a year)

Extra return = return above safe US Treasury bills. Market premium = the extra return of the whole US stock market since 1963.

If Sharpe is right, the data should show three things

Prediction 1

Return rises with beta, in a straight line

Each extra unit of beta should earn the full market premium: 7.2% a year.

Prediction 2

No market risk, no extra return

A portfolio with a beta of zero should earn 0% above T-bills, and no portfolio should beat its CAPM prediction (alpha = 0).

Prediction 3

In bad times, high beta hurts most

Sharpe calls low-beta assets "defensive" and high-beta assets "aggressive": in recessions, aggressive sectors should fall the most.

How we test it: US stocks sorted into 10 portfolios by beta (D1 lowest, D10 highest), 759 months of data, regressions in Python, SQL and R.

The research

The test: 10 portfolios sorted by beta

Testing predictions 1 and 2. US stocks are sorted into 10 portfolios, from the lowest beta (D1) to the highest (D10). If the CAPM is right, their returns sit on the dashed line. They don't: the actual line is half as steep and starts well above zero.

01

Low beta earns too much. D1 beats its CAPM prediction by 2.4% a year (t = 2.48, significant).

02

High beta earns too little. D10 has 2.7 times the beta of D1, but earns only about 2 points more.

03

Joint test. GRS = 1.78, p = 0.061: the 10 alphas are jointly non-zero at the 10% level, not at 5%.

Data: Kenneth R. French Data Library, value-weighted portfolios, 1963-07 to 2026-09. Same results in R, Databricks SQL and Python.

Defensive vs aggressive

Which sectors hold up in recessions?

Testing prediction 3. In the 85 months of NBER recessions, the market lost 7.4% a year over the risk-free rate. Retail, healthcare and consumer staples held up; energy and manufacturing fell hardest. This is Sharpe's split between "defensive" and "aggressive" assets.

How the signals work
  • Market regime (S&P 500): Risk-on above its 200-day average and less than 10% below its 12-month high; Caution below its 200-day average; Correction 10 to 20% below its high; Bear market more than 20% below.
  • Style (1-year beta): Defensive below 0.9, Aggressive above 1.1, as in Sharpe (1964, p. 441-442).
  • Risk-on: follow the trend. Overweight a sector above its 200-day average that beat the market over 3 months.
  • Any other regime: protect the portfolio. Favour defensive sectors, which held up best in past recessions.

Rule-based signals built for a class project. Not investment advice.

Conclusion

So, does risk pay?

Three predictions, three verdicts.

Sharpe predictsWhat the data showVerdict
1. Return rises with beta, by 7.2% a year per unit Return rises with beta, but only by 3.5% a year per unit: the line is half as steep Partly
2. Zero beta earns 0%, every alpha is 0 Zero beta earns 4.3%; low-beta stocks earn 2.4% a year too much (significant); GRS p = 0.061 Rejected
3. In recessions, aggressive sectors fall the most Energy -14.0% and manufacturing -11.2% a year, while retail still earns +6.1% Confirmed

What we proved

More risk brings more return, but only half as much as the theory says. Low-risk stocks are a better deal than the CAPM predicts, and risky sectors are hit hardest in a crisis.

Why? Sharpe himself called his assumptions "highly restrictive". He assumed everyone can borrow at the risk-free rate. In reality, investors who want more return often buy high-beta stocks instead of borrowing, which pushes their prices up and their future returns down (Black, 1972; Frazzini and Pedersen, 2014).
